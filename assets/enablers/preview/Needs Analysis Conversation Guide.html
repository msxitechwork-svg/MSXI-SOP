Needs Analysis Conversation Guide for Sales Consultants
1. Purpose and approach
Use the questionnaire as a capture sheet—not a script to read aloud. The customer should feel that you are understanding their requirements, not conducting an interview.
Follow this conversational sequence:
Who will use the vehicle → How it will be used → What matters most → Current vehicle and alternatives → Budget and purchase arrangements → Timing and recap
If the customer answers a later question naturally, record it then. Do not ask it again simply to follow the sequence.
2. Start with a welcoming introduction
Suggested opening
“Before we look at the car in detail, may I understand a little about what you’re looking for? That will help me focus on the things that matter most to you.”
Keep the questionnaire available for reference, but maintain eye contact. If entering responses on a device, explain briefly:
“I’ll make a few notes so I can keep your preferences in mind.”
3. Recommended conversation flow
The sequence below covers all 12 questionnaire items, arranged to make the conversation feel natural.
Phase A: Understand the customer’s everyday use
Question 1: Primary user of the vehicle
Say:
“Who will mainly be using the car—you or someone in the family?
Capture: Self / Family member
Conversation guidance:
Acknowledge the answer without making assumptions. A chauffeur-driven customer may still value driving performance; a self-driving customer may also prioritise rear-seat comfort.
Bridge:
“And what would the car mainly be used for?”
Question 2: Primary purpose
Say:
“Would it mainly be for the daily commute, weekend drives, or a mix of everything?”
Capture: Daily commute / Weekend drives / All-purpose
Conversation guidance:
Let the customer describe their routine in their own words, then select the suitable option. Do not force them to choose before they have finished explaining the purpose of vehicle
Bridge:
“To get an understanding of vehicle usage”
Question 3: Typical daily driving distance
Say:
“On a typical day, roughly how many kilometres would the car cover?”
Capture: Daily distance using the km slider
Conversation guidance:
An approximate distance is sufficient. however clarify with customer that you mean the total distance for the day, including the return journey
If usage varies considerably, confirm a daily KM figure with the customer rather than assuming one by yourself.
Bridge:
“That helps me understand your driving pattern. Since we’re discussing a PHEV”
Phase B: Explore awareness and priorities
Question 4: Familiarity with PHEV technology
Say:
“How familiar are you with plug-in hybrid, or PHEV technology? Is it fine if we provide you a quick introduction about it?”
Capture: The customer’s stated level of PHEV familiarity, using the questionnaire’s available response format.
Conversation guidance:
Keep the tone interactive and not like a knowledge test.
If unfamiliar: “A PHEV combines an electric motor and a battery that can be charged externally with a combustion engine.”
If somewhat familiar: Acknowledge what they already know and clarify any questions.
If very familiar: Avoid repeating a basic explanation; move on to their priorities.
Use verified, model-specific information for any discussion of range, charging, fuel consumption or operating modes.
Bridge:
“Thinking about your next car overall…”
Question 5: Top three attributes
Say:
“Which three attributes matter most to you: technology, design, safety, comfort, or performance?”
Capture: Three selections from Technology / Design / Safety / Comfort / Performance
Conversation guidance:
If the customer says “all of them,” respond:
“Of course—they’re all important. If you had to choose the three that you would want us to focus on during the demonstration, which would they be?”
Reflect their choices back:
“So your main priorities are [attribute1], [attribute2] and [attribute3]. I’ll keep those in focus when we walk around the car.”
Bridge:
“To understand what you’re comparing this with…”
Phase C: Understand their current vehicle and alternatives
Question 6: Current primary vehicle
Say:
“What do you currently drive or use as your main car?”
Capture: Brand and model
Conversation guidance:
If the answer is vague, gently clarify the brand and model. If the customer does not currently have a vehicle, record that accurately
Bridge:
“And as you explore your next car…”
Question 7: Other brands or models under consideration
Say:
“Are there any other brands or models you’re considering or explored?”
Capture: Brand and model of the alternative brands mentioned
Conversation guidance:
Stay neutral and acknowledge the customer’s shortlist. Do not criticise competing brands or make unverified comparisons.
Bridge:
If they have a current vehicle:
“And what are you planning to do with your current car?”
Question 8: Interest in exchanging the current vehicle
Say:
“Would you be interested in exploring an exchange for your current vehicle?”
Capture: Yes / No
Conversation guidance:
Treat “Yes” as interest in an exchange assessment—not a commitment to exchange. Do not promise a valuation.
If the customer has no current vehicle, acknowledge that the exchange question does not apply and record it according to the system
Bridge:
“To make sure the options we discuss fit your plans…”
Phase D: Discuss budget and purchase arrangements
Question 9: Budget range
Say:
“What budget range are you considering—₹15–25 lakh, ₹25–35 lakh, or above ₹35 lakh?”
Capture: ₹15–25 lakh / ₹25–35 lakh / above ₹35 lakh
Conversation guidance:
Ask matter-of-factly, without judgement or pressure. If needed, clarify whether the customer means on-road or ex-showroom pricing, and note the basis.
If the customer is undecided, outside the listed ranges, or gives an amount at a shared boundary, clarify their intended selection rather than assigning a bracket yourself.
Bridge:
“And for the purchase arrangements…”
Question 10: Registration in whose name
Say:
“Would the car be registered in an individual’s name or a company’s name?”
Capture: Individual / Company
Conversation guidance:
Keep this as an administrative preference question. Do not imply tax benefits or eligibility without verification.
Bridge:
“Along with that…”
Question 11: Financing assistance
Say:
“Would you like assistance exploring finance options?”
Capture: Yes / No
Conversation guidance:
A “Yes” indicates interest in assistance, not a finance application or approval. Avoid requesting detailed financial information during this initial conversation unless the customer wants to proceed.
Bridge:
So I can suggest the right next step…”
Question 12: Planned purchase timing
Say:
“When are you hoping to purchase—immediately, within 30–60 days, or are you still deciding?”
Capture: Immediately / Within 30–60 days / Undecided
Conversation guidance:
Accept the answer without creating pressure. If their actual timeline does not fit the choices, note it and clarify how it should be captured.
Close the discussion with a personalised recap
Summarise what the customer has actually told you. Do not read all 12 answers back to back
Suggested closing
“Let me check that I’ve understood correctly. The car will mainly be used by [primary user] for [purpose], covering around [distance] kilometres a day. Your main priorities are [three attributes], and you’re considering [budget range], with a purchase planned [timing]. Have I understood that correctly?”
After confirmation:
“Thank you. Let’s start the demonstration with the areas you’ve highlighted.”
Where relevant, briefly acknowledge exchange or financing interest as part of the next steps.
5. Essential consultant guidelines
Do
Ask one question at a time and listen fully.
Use the customer’s previous answer to introduce and link the next topic.
Record information already shared voluntarily.
Acknowledge answers naturally and briefly.
Capture uncertainty honestly and clarify only where needed.
Use the selected priorities to personalise the demonstration.
Avoid
Reading out the form as a rapid checklist.
Using the same transition regardless of their answer.
Asking the customer to repeat an answer already given.
Responding to every answer with a sales pitch.
Testing the customer’s technical knowledge.
Guessing, forcing a selection or treating interest as commitment.
Gathering information and then delivering a generic presentation.
